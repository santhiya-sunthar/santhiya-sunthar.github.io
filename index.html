Capstone summary · Toronto Metropolitan University · 2023

What 165,000 Clicks Taught Me About Online Shoppers

I studied five months of browsing data from an online maternity clothing store to learn how shoppers move through the site. This was my final project for the Big Data Analytics program at Toronto Metropolitan University in 2023.

The project

Every time a shopper clicks on a product, the website records it. The store's records covered about 24,000 shopping visits and 217 products across trousers, skirts, blouses and sale items. I asked three questions:

  1. Which products do people look at together?
  2. Can we sort shoppers into groups with similar habits?
  3. Can a product's features tell us whether it will be priced high or low?

What I found

Shoppers tend to stay in one aisle. Most people browsed products from the same category, and some pairs were viewed together again and again. Certain sale items were over 20 times more likely to be viewed together than by chance. A store could use these pairings for "you may also like" suggestions or bundle deals.

Shoppers fall into four groups. They range from quick visitors who glance at a few items to dedicated shoppers who browse many higher-priced products. Each group could get its own messaging, such as bestsellers for quick visitors and premium bundles for dedicated ones.

A perfect score was too good to be true. One of my pricing models was right 100% of the time. When I dug in, I found it was effectively being handed the answer, because each product code already came with a fixed price. Once that shortcut was gone, the models did no better than a coin flip. The lesson I took away: a result that looks too good deserves as much scrutiny as one that looks bad.

What I'd do next

With richer data, such as purchases, time spent on each page and product photos, I could link browsing to actual sales. That would show which habits lead to revenue, not just clicks.

R, Python and SAS.
